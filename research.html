Dissecting Tree of Thoughts, an Ablation Study of ToT Search Algorithms

Jaden Cutinha & Junze Xie · Princeton University

overview

Large language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse reasoning tasks, yet they often struggle with problems requiring planning, exploration, or backtracking. Tree of Thoughts, introduced by Yao et al. (2023), addresses this limitation by framing problem solving as a search over a tree of intermediate reasoning steps. The original method relies on breadth first search and depth first search, relatively simple algorithms that do not fully exploit accumulated information about the search frontier.

This paper asks a focused question. If the improvements of Tree of Thoughts over chain of thought prompting stem from deliberate search, would more informed search strategies yield further gains? We replace the original breadth first and depth first search with k beam A* search, which treats the problem as a shortest path search using model value scores as a heuristic, and Monte Carlo Tree Search, which treats it as a sequential decision problem with simulated rollouts and exploration in the style of upper confidence bounds. Every other variable, meaning the generation method, the evaluation method, the proposal completions, and the value samples, is held fixed so that the search algorithm is the sole independent variable.

methods

Experiments were conducted on two standard benchmarks, the Game of 24 and 5×5 mini crosswords, using a GPT-4o-mini backbone. Puzzles were drawn from fixed index ranges to ensure reproducibility. Each trial logged accuracy, API call count, and wall clock time. For A*, the max heap size was set to 7 and expansion was capped at 10 counted steps for mini crosswords. Beam width was k = 5 for A* and breadth first search. Monte Carlo Tree Search used three value rollouts.

key results

19%
A* accuracy on the Game of 24
16%
Monte Carlo Tree Search accuracy on the Game of 24
8%
Breadth first search accuracy on the Game of 24

On the Game of 24, A* significantly outperformed breadth first search while using a nearly identical number of API calls, 89.66 against 86.24 per trial, demonstrating that heuristic guided global ordering delivers meaningful gains at no extra cost. Monte Carlo Tree Search achieved 16% accuracy at only 64.9 API calls per trial, the most efficient configuration. On mini crosswords, all three methods converged to similar accuracy near 20 to 26 percent, because tight search budgets prevent any method from escaping a weak early word choice.

takeaways

The advantage of A* on the Game of 24 is attributable to its global best first ordering combined with value scores averaged along the path, which systematically allocates search effort toward the most promising trajectories. The strict pruning of breadth first search at each layer loses recoverable paths that a globally ordered frontier would have found. Monte Carlo Tree Search, by running random rollouts to the horizon and backing up scores as prefix support, gives paths a second chance, explaining why it exceeds breadth first search but falls short of the systematic ordering that A* provides. These findings suggest that more informative search is a genuine lever for improving reasoning performance.

Evaluating AI Sentiment Consistency and Market Relevance

Jaden Cutinha · Computer Science, Princeton University, Class of 2028

research question

Can AI reliably interpret ambiguous financial news, and does that interpretation help predict the stock market? This project examines whether leading AI models agree on sentiment and whether their judgments correlate with next day market movements, addressing a gap between AI sentiment research and practical financial signal generation.

methods

Two language models, GPT-4o and Llama 3.3 70B Instruct, were tested on 45 ambiguous corporate financial headlines drawn from 2023 and 2024. Headlines were defined as ambiguous, meaning corporate events with no clearly positive or negative valence. Both models used identical deterministic prompts at temperature zero, and their sentiment labels of positive, neutral, and negative were compared across the full sample. Labels were then matched to next day stock returns to evaluate whether AI sentiment aligns with real market behavior. An OLS regression was run with the GPT-4o sentiment score as predictor and the next day percentage return as the dependent variable.

key results

29%
Disagreement rate between the two models
κ = 0.55
Cohen's Kappa, indicating moderate agreement
R² = 0.17
Fit of sentiment against next day return

The models disagreed on approximately 30% of headlines, indicating that AI financial sentiment is not a fully objective signal. Disagreements were most common for headlines involving strategic shifts and pricing changes, where the direction of the implication is inherently open to interpretation. GPT-4o sentiment showed a statistically detectable but weak relationship with next day returns, with R² = 0.170 at p = 0.206, suggesting the effect size is insufficient for standalone predictive use.

conclusions & implications

Language models exhibit meaningful disagreement with one another when classifying ambiguous financial sentiment, indicating that model outputs reflect the interpretation of a particular model rather than an objective signal. While there is a detectable directional relationship between AI sentiment and next day returns, it falls short of statistical significance at standard thresholds. These findings suggest that AI sentiment classification should be treated as one noisy input among many, rather than a reliable standalone directional indicator. Future work should scale the dataset, incorporate intraday data, and test models built specifically for finance, and could explore combining AI sentiment with traditional financial indicators for stronger predictive performance.
